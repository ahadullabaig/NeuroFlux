\begin{abstract}
Spiking neural networks suit always-on biosignal processing, but how
faithfully they reproduce an analog signal is rarely quantified. Feed-forward
leaky integrate-and-fire networks have a fidelity ceiling: on 16 MIT-BIH ECG
records and three synthetic signals, output SNR rises by less than 0.1 dB per
dB of input SNR above 10 dB, under three spike codes. Given the ceiling from
one noiseless run at the operating point, an inverse-SNR cascade law of
Friis's form predicts the output on real ECG to 0.04 dB RMS over a 60 dB input
range, with no fitted parameters. Noise that neurons share sets the ceiling:
width and causal readout windows do not raise it, and STDP lowers it while
collapsing effective fan-in from 24 inputs to about 3. In the networks tested,
a fixed fan-in of 16 raises it by 1.85 dB, to 5.77 dB, with 32 times fewer
synapses. A standard ECG filter still recovers heartbeats better than any
configuration: these are limits and design rules for feed-forward spiking
signal chains, not a denoiser.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Neuromorphic processors run spiking neural networks (SNNs) with sparse,
event-driven computation~\cite{davies2018loihi}, which suits always-on
biosignal processing. Most SNN studies of the electrocardiogram (ECG) target
arrhythmia classification~\cite{choi2024biomedical}; those that return a signal
are trained with supervision and avoid rate coding at the input, the output or
both~\cite{ren2026dcpa,henkes2024regression}, and a rate-coded SNN scored
almost 8~dB below other spiking variants at image
denoising~\cite{castagnetti2023spiden}. Noise shared across neurons limits what
averaging a population
recovers~\cite{zohary1994correlated,morenobote2014information,kanitscheider2015origin},
rates and synchrony degrade or stabilize across layers of noisy
neurons~\cite{diesmann1999stable,vanrossum2002fast}, and sparse connectivity
has an optimal degree~\cite{litwinkumar2017optimal}. We know of no work that
turns these into a stage-by-stage fidelity budget for a spiking signal chain.

When a rate-calibrated network of leaky integrate-and-fire (LIF) neurons
reconstructs a noisy ECG, raising the input signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) from 20
to 40\dB{}, a hundredfold cut in noise power, lifts the output by about
0.1\dB. We call this plateau the \emph{fidelity ceiling}. The network is not a
competitive denoiser (Section~\ref{sec:discussion}), so we ask what sets the
ceiling. For feed-forward LIF networks in simulation we show that (i)~it is
flat in input SNR on real and synthetic ECG, a synthetic electroencephalogram
(EEG) and a sine, under three spike codes, and width, causal readout windows
and spike-timing-dependent plasticity (STDP) do not raise it; (ii)~a cascade
law of Friis's form predicts output SNR on real ECG to 0.04\dB{} RMS, with no
fitted parameters given the ceiling from one noiseless run; and (iii)~the
binding noise is shared across neurons: in the networks tested a fixed fan-in
of 8--16 raises the ceiling by up to 1.85\dB{} with 16--32 times fewer
synapses, while STDP lowers it by collapsing fan-in.

\section{Pipeline and Measurement}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{Network}
A scalar signal sampled at 250~Hz (time step 4~ms) is copied to 32 Poisson
encoders, each firing with probability proportional to the amplitude
normalized to the drive's own minimum and maximum. Two all-to-all layers of 16
and 8 LIF neurons follow ($[32,16,8]$, Fig.~\ref{fig:pipeline}; rest
$-70$~mV, threshold $-55$~mV, reset $-80$~mV, membrane time constant 20~ms,
refractory period 2~ms), with nonnegative weights uniform on $[0, 0.3]$. The
output is the mean of the output neurons' exponentially filtered spike trains
(time constant 20~ms), a causal readout. Where stated, pair-based trace STDP
(time constants 20~ms, weights in $[0,1]$), as used for label-free SNN
learning~\cite{diehl2015unsupervised}, trains the network on the noisy input.

Output quality depends strongly on firing rate, so the encoder's maximum rate
and each layer's synaptic gain are bisected until every stage fires at 40~Hz
on the drive, as in SNN rate
normalization~\cite{diehl2015balancing,rueckauer2017conversion}; trained
networks are recalibrated after learning, so that only their weights differ.
We also report the network as shipped: weights uniform on $[0,1]$, one global
gain and free rates.

\subsection{Signals}
Real ECG comes from 16 records of the MIT-BIH Arrhythmia
Database~\cite{moody2001impact,goldberger2000physionet}, listed with the code,
a different artifact-free 10~s window per seed, resampled from 360 to 250~Hz.
Three 10~s synthetic signals are added: an ECG at 60~beats/min, an EEG of 10,
20 and 40~Hz components with random phases, and a 2~Hz sine. Gaussian noise
is scaled so that the input SNR against the clean signal equals its nominal
value exactly, from $-20$ to $+40$\dB{} in 5\dB{} steps plus a noiseless
input. Each condition runs with 10 seeds.

\subsection{Fidelity metric}
A readout's gain and offset are arbitrary, so we fit $\hat{x} = ay + b$ to the
clean signal $x$ by least squares and score the affine-corrected
$\mathrm{SNR} = \mathrm{var}(x)/\mathrm{var}(x-\hat{x})$ in dB. For a signal
plus independent noise this is $1/(1-\rho^2) = 1 + \SN$, with $\rho$ the
correlation and $\SN$ the signal-to-noise power ratio, S/N. The
\emph{ceiling} is the output SNR at noiseless input. Runs with a constant
output, or with half the output neurons at 90\% of the maximum rate or more,
are excluded; only trained shipped networks and networks held at 0.25~Hz ever
were. SNRs are causal unless \emph{delay-compensated}: output advanced by its
best lag in 0--64~ms.

\subsection{Statistics}
The unit of analysis is a record ($n = 16$) or, for synthetic signals, a seed
($n = 10$). Paired comparisons use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Holm
correction within each family and report the median difference with a
bias-corrected and accelerated (BCa) bootstrap 95\% interval (10\,000
resamples). Invariance is claimed only by equivalence (two one-sided tests,
margin $\pm 0.5$\dB{}). Tests and margins for the main comparisons were fixed
before their grids ran; later checks are labeled post hoc. More than
65\,000 simulations underlie the results; code, configurations and results are
at \url{https://github.com/ahadullabaig/NeuroFlux}.

\section{A Cascade Law for Spiking Stages}
\label{sec:law}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering

  arr/.style={-{Latex[length=1.5mm,width=1.2mm]}, semithick},
  noise/.style={-{Latex[length=1.3mm,width=1mm]}, thin},

}
\end{tikzpicture}
\caption{The pipeline and its noise model. Each stage adds its own
spike-sampling noise $\eta_k$, of intrinsic S/N $\Lambda_k$, so inverse S/N
ratios add~\eqref{eq:law}.}
\label{fig:pipeline}
\end{figure}

Write stage $k$'s readout as what it inherits plus the noise it adds,
$y_k = G_k\, y_{k-1} + \eta_k$, with $\eta_k$ its own spike-sampling noise,
independent of upstream noise. The gain scales signal and inherited noise
alike and cancels from their ratio, so $1/\SN_k = 1/\SN_{k-1} + 1/\Lambda_k$,
where $\Lambda_k = G_k^2 S_{k-1}/\mathrm{var}(\eta_k)$ is the stage's
\emph{intrinsic} S/N and $S_{k-1}$ the signal power reaching it. Summing over
stages,
\begin{equation}
\frac{1}{\SN_\mathrm{out}} = \frac{1}{\SN_\mathrm{in}} + \sum_k
\frac{1}{\Lambda_k}, \qquad
C = \Bigl(\sum_k \frac{1}{\Lambda_k}\Bigr)^{-1},
\label{eq:law}
\end{equation}
the form of Friis's cascade formula for amplifier
noise~\cite{friis1944noise}, in inverse S/N. As $\SN_\mathrm{in} \to \infty$
the output tends to $C$, the ceiling, set by the network alone; every added
stage adds a positive term, and improving a stage whose $1/\Lambda_k$ is a
small part of the sum does little.

Two conditions make \eqref{eq:law} accurate to hundredths of a dB on ECG and
EEG. It holds for $\SN$, not for the affine-corrected $\mathrm{SNR} = 1 + \SN$,
which must be converted first, or the law over-predicts loss by about 2\dB{}
below 10\dB{} input. And $C$ must be measured at the input's operating point:
the encoder's range and the gains follow the noisy drive, so for each input
level $C$ comes from a noiseless run at the operating point that level's drive
sets. The law thus has no free parameter, but it relates measurements: $C$
comes from simulation, not from the architecture alone, and only the
encoder's term has a closed form (Section~\ref{sec:flat}).

\emph{Where $\Lambda$ comes from.} Averaging $N$ neurons whose readout errors
have variance $\sigma_1^2$ and mean pairwise correlation $c$ gives
\begin{equation}
\Lambda(N) = \frac{\sigma_s^2}{\sigma_1^2\,[\,1/N + c\,(1-1/N)\,]}
\;\xrightarrow{N\to\infty}\; \frac{\sigma_s^2}{c\,\sigma_1^2},
\label{eq:width}
\end{equation}
with $\sigma_s^2$ the signal variance in one neuron's trace: shared error caps
what width can buy, the classic limit on averaging correlated
neurons~\cite{zohary1994correlated}. At a noiseless input the encoders fire
independently, so this correlation is made by the network, not copied from the
input; with its own noise on each input channel, width gains only 0.09\dB{}
per doubling on MIT-BIH, 2.8\% of what independent samples allow. Drive noise
is correlated through the overlap of input
filters~\cite{kanitscheider2015origin}: for independent, equal-variance
inputs, by the cosine similarity of the weight columns, $\mu^2/(\mu^2+\sigma^2)$
for independent weights of mean $\mu$ and variance $\sigma^2$. Any uniform
draw on $[0,a]$ gives $3/4$, so rescaling weights, as rate calibration does,
cannot change it. Fewer inputs per neuron lower the overlap but leave fewer
spike trains to average, so fan-in should have an optimum.

\section{Results}

\subsection{The ceiling is flat, and the law predicts it}
\label{sec:flat}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig1_ceiling_law.pdf}
\caption{Rate-controlled network, untrained. (a, b) Output SNR against input
SNR: markers are medians over records or seeds, lines the cascade
law~\eqref{eq:law} with the ceiling measured at each input's operating point,
the grey line the unprocessed input, and the filled markers at $\infty$ the
noiseless ceiling with its interquartile range. (c) Measured minus predicted
output SNR; dashed, the law with the ordinary noiseless ceiling.}
\label{fig:ceiling}
\end{figure}

With every layer at 40~Hz, output SNR saturates on all four signals
(Fig.~\ref{fig:ceiling}a, b): above 10\dB{}, where the input's own SNR rises
0.99\dB{} per dB, the output rises $+0.012$ [$+0.005$, $+0.014$]\dB{} per dB on
MIT-BIH and at most $+0.092$ (sine), every 95\% interval excluding~1, and the
shipped network, untrained or trained, is as flat (slopes at most 0.07). The
ceiling is 1.92\dB{} on MIT-BIH (interquartile range 1.38--2.27), 1.76 on
synthetic ECG, 2.29 on EEG and 7.48 on the sine.

The law predicts the MIT-BIH output with an RMS residual of 0.02\dB{} at
inputs of 10\dB{} and above and 0.04\dB{} below, at most 0.11\dB{} over all 208
record-level points (Fig.~\ref{fig:ceiling}c), and to 0.04--0.07\dB{} RMS on
synthetic ECG and EEG. The sine, the highest-fidelity case (ceiling S/N 4.6,
against 0.56 on MIT-BIH), fits less well: RMS 0.18--0.23\dB, with an
unexplained bias of up to $+0.3$\dB{} between 0 and 10\dB{} input. Without the
operating-point match, a check designed after we saw the ordinary residual,
the MIT-BIH residual below 10\dB{} grows to 0.39\dB{} RMS (dashed): noise
widens the encoder's range and squeezes the signal into less of it, at
0\dB{} input alone costing 1.1\dB{} of ceiling.

\emph{Independent noise, and what sets the height} (post hoc). The encoder's
noise is exactly Bernoulli shot noise, so
$1/\SN_0 = 1/F + 1/(\rho^2\Lambda_\mathrm{s})$, with $F$ the S/N the 20~ms
readout filter alone keeps, $\rho$ its correlation with $x$ and
$\Lambda_\mathrm{s}$ the shot-noise S/N of the 32 encoders, all from the clean
signal. With nothing fitted this predicts the measured encoder stage to
0.11\dB{} RMS over the 16 records; its 0.2--0.4\dB{} excess on EEG and the
sine is the readout's clip at the maximum rate. With no spikes, the causal
filter alone would cap MIT-BIH at 5.5\dB, EEG at 2.7 and the sine at 13.0,
mostly through its delay (9.9, 8.9 and 31.7\dB{} delay-compensated). Of
MIT-BIH's $1/C$ the filter takes 21\%, encoder spikes 12\% and the LIF layers
67\%; on EEG (10--40~Hz) the filter takes 82\%, mostly through its lag. The
sine's encoder noise is lower, its shot noise alone capping it at 14.9\dB{}
against 8.0 on MIT-BIH, because a smooth signal fills its encoder range
(squared coefficient of variation 0.50, against 0.22 for ECG, whose variance
sits in brief QRS complexes).

\subsection{What moves the ceiling}
\label{sec:levers}

\input{figures/table1_levers.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:levers} tests each design choice on MIT-BIH against the
reference network in its second column. \emph{Width}: 16 times the output
population buys $+0.08$\dB{} and every width from 4 to 64 is equivalent;
independent neurons would have gained $+0.79$\dB{} by~\eqref{eq:width} with
$c=0$, and the fitted $c$ is 0.48. \emph{Readout}: windows of 5, 10 and 160~ms
cost 0.75--1.06\dB{} against the 20--40~ms optimum. \emph{Coding}: a
time-to-first-spike code read by first-spike latency raises the ceiling on
none of the four signals, even delay-compensated; a delta code calibrated to
40~Hz follows $|dx/dt|$ rather than $x$ and scores significantly lower on both
ECGs and the sine; and for all three codes, four signals and both output rates
the slope above 10\dB{} stays below 1 (24 of 24 cells).

\emph{Depth and learning} lower the ceiling, as~\eqref{eq:law} requires of
depth: 2, 3 and 4 layers give 2.75, 1.92 and 1.57\dB, and 10 and 200 STDP
epochs with rates held cost 0.49 and 0.64\dB. \emph{Output rate} is the
largest operating-point lever: with 64 output neurons at 5~Hz the ceiling is
2.71\dB{} against 1.99 at 40~Hz, with eight times fewer output spikes. A
sparse output acts as an event detector, which suits a signal whose variance
sits in brief landmarks (post hoc): eight records gain 1.2--1.9\dB{} at 5~Hz
and the other eight, six of them 200-series arrhythmia records, $-0.8$ to
$+0.5$\dB; EEG is flat from 10 to 40~Hz and the sine peaks at 40~Hz.

\subsection{Mechanism: shared error and fan-in}
\label{sec:mechanism}

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{fig2_fan_in.pdf}
\caption{Fan-in. (a) MIT-BIH, medians over 16 records: one-layer $[N, N]$
networks at each held firing rate, each neuron drawing exactly $K$ random
inputs (solid) or all $N$ (dashed). (b) Layer-1 effective inputs,
$(\sum_i w_i)^2/\sum_i w_i^2$ per weight column, against the ceiling of the
rate-controlled $[32,16,8]$ network after 0, 10, 50 and 200 STDP epochs;
medians over records or seeds.}
\label{fig:fanin}
\end{figure}

We tested the trade-off in one-layer $[N,N]$ networks whose neurons each draw
exactly $K$ random inputs, with depth, width, code and firing rate fixed so
that only fan-in changes; across widths, fan-in and column cosine ($3K/4N$)
vary separately. At 40~Hz the ceiling peaks at $K = 8$--16 at every width from
32 to 512, beating dense weights in all 16 records, and the error correlation
tracks column cosine ($R^2 = 0.85$): the optimal-degree trade-off known for
expansion layers~\cite{litwinkumar2017optimal}, here for reconstruction. The
gain holds at every firing rate from 1 to 40~Hz (Fig.~\ref{fig:fanin}a;
Wilcoxon $p < 10^{-4}$ at each rate). At each configuration's best rate,
$K = 16$ in $[512,512]$ reaches 5.77\dB{}, $+1.85$ [$+1.38$, $+2.26$]\dB{} over
dense weights and five times the reference network's S/N (2.78 against 0.56),
and $K = 8$ in $[128,128]$ gains $+1.10$ [$+0.73$, $+1.57$]\dB{}, with 32 and 16
times fewer synapses and synaptic events.

STDP falls off the other side of the same trade-off. On the tiled input it
depresses most of each layer-1 neuron's synapses: the effective number of
inputs falls from 24.3 to 5.1, 3.0 and 2.6 at 10, 50 and 200 epochs
(Fig.~\ref{fig:fanin}b). Across records and epoch counts the drop tracks the
loss of layer-1 intrinsic S/N (Spearman $\rho = +0.79$, $p = 4\times10^{-11}$,
$n = 48$), whereas the change in column cosine does not ($\rho = +0.11$,
$p = 0.45$): training in fact \emph{decorrelates} the columns (cosine 0.76 to
0.38), but three Poisson trains average far worse than~24. Fixing $K$ is an
intervention, so fan-in sets the ceiling causally; that STDP acts through
fan-in rests on these correlations, on one signal copied to every input, and
is untested on non-redundant inputs.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\emph{Design rules.} Like a radio receiver chain, a spiking pipeline can be
budgeted stage by stage: one noiseless run at the operating point gives each
$\Lambda_k$, and \eqref{eq:law} the output at any input. For the networks and
signals studied here, not SNNs in general: once the sensor's S/N is ten times
the ceiling, improving it adds at most 0.4\dB{} of output S/N; removing one of
three spiking layers gains 0.91\dB; 8--16 inputs per neuron gain up to
1.85\dB{} with 16--32 times fewer synapses; the output rate should match the
signal (5~Hz beats 40~Hz for ECG, not for a sine); and an encoder range taken
from a 40~Hz low-pass copy of the input adds 0.35\dB{} on MIT-BIH at 0\dB{}
input (below the 0.5\dB{} bar we set in advance) and 1.0\dB{} on the sine.

\emph{Implications for devices.} Memristive synapses cannot hold negative
conductances, so signed weights need differential device
pairs~\cite{prezioso2015training}, and dense, nonnegative, random connectivity
is exactly the regime of 3/4 column overlap. By~\eqref{eq:width}, sparse
fan-in, measured here, or zero-mean signed weights, untested and at twice the
devices, would reduce it; whether fixing fan-in during on-chip learning
prevents STDP's loss is untested.

\emph{Limitations.} The conclusions apply only to the class simulated:
feed-forward LIF networks without inhibition, recurrence or trained readouts,
fed one signal copied across the inputs, with real data from one database.
Every ceiling here is causal; removing the readout's delay offline (post hoc)
raises the best MIT-BIH ceiling from 1.98 to 2.86\dB, still flat, and whether
the law holds as closely there is untested. Clinically, the network does not
compete with conventional preprocessing: scored against the annotations with
three QRS detectors in NeuroKit2~\cite{makowski2021neurokit2}, the noisy input
after the toolbox's standard cleaning filters beats the best network condition
significantly in 16 of 18 input and detector combinations, and loses in none.

\section{Conclusion}

Feed-forward LIF networks for biosignal processing have a fidelity ceiling,
flat in input quality across three spike codes and four signals, that a
Friis-type cascade law predicts to hundredths of a dB on ECG and EEG once one
noiseless run has measured it. Error that neurons share and the readout's
delay set it; width and learning do not raise it, and in the networks tested a
fixed fan-in of 8--16 does, with fewer synapses. These are limits and design
rules, not a denoiser.
